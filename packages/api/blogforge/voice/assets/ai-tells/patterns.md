- Rule: **Negation / antithesis.** Do not set up a contrast just to knock it down. Avoid "It's not just X, it's Y", "not only A but also B", "X isn't just about Y", and "X is more than just Y"; state the affirmative directly. Before: "This isn't a tool, it's a movement." After: "This tool cuts deploy time from an hour to a minute."
  Because: Formulaic contrast is a recurrent style defect that makes prose sound machine-generated.
- Rule: **Don't dodge plain verbs.** Use "is" and "has" instead of inflating them into "serves as", "stands as", "boasts", "represents a", "plays a vital role", or "leaves an indelible mark." Before: "The API serves as a gateway that boasts low latency." After: "The API is the gateway. It adds 5ms."
  Because: Inflated verbs obscure the point and read like a model-generated flourish.
- Rule: **Don't force the rule of three.** Use a triplet only when all three terms earn their place; otherwise one strong term wins.
  Because: Padding ideas into a stock triplet is a recurrent AI-writing tell.
- Rule: **No participle tack-ons.** Do not append fake significance with "-ing" clauses such as "highlighting its importance", "creating a seamless experience", or "reflecting a broader trend." End the sentence.
  Because: These tack-ons add generic emphasis without advancing the reader's understanding.
- Rule: **No puffery.** Drop travel-brochure and press-release adjectives such as "nestled in the heart of", "rich tapestry", "vibrant community", and "breathtaking." Name the specific thing instead.
  Because: Specific details are more useful to readers than generic promotional language.
- Rule: **No vague attribution.** Do not cite "experts", "studies show", "industry reports", or "observers note" without a real source. Name it or cut it.
  Because: Unsupported attribution damages factual trust and makes the claim unreliable.
- Rule: **Synthesize, don't summarize.** Connect ideas and show what they mean or why they matter instead of merely listing them one after another.
  Because: Readers need the argument, not a sequence of disconnected summary points.
- Rule: **Skip generic praise adjectives.** Replace words such as "significant", "important", "effective", and "essential" with the specific reason or number that makes the point.
  Because: Concrete support helps readers understand why the claim matters.
- Rule: **Vary sentence length (burstiness).** Do not write three same-length sentences in a row; mix a short blunt sentence with a longer one that carries a couple of clauses.
  Because: A uniform metronomic rhythm is a recognizable machine-writing tell.
- Rule: Do not use em dashes or en dashes. **No em-dash overuse.** Recast with a period, comma, colon, or parentheses.
  Because: This text will be read by a text-to-speech engine, and disruptive punctuation can produce confusing pauses or phrasing.
- Rule: **Don't bridge with rhetorical questions.** Avoid the self-posed-question-then-answer move, such as "The result? Faster builds." or "Why does this matter? Because…" State the point directly.
  Because: This framing pattern is a recurrent style defect that makes prose sound machine-generated.
- Rule: **Skip the countdown.** Do not negate options for drama, such as "Not a bug. Not a feature. A design flaw." State the affirmative.
  Because: Dramatic countdowns are a recognizable formula rather than a clear explanation.
- Rule: **No anaphora padding.** Do not open several sentences in a row with the same word, such as "They assume… They assume… They assume…" Vary the openings or combine the sentences.
  Because: Repeated openings create a mechanical cadence.
- Rule: **No false ranges.** Use "from X to Y to Z" only when the items sit on a real spectrum; do not connect unrelated things such as "from databases to team culture" for sweep.
  Because: False ranges substitute a model-like flourish for a real relationship between ideas.
- Rule: **Repeat the clear word; don't cycle synonyms.** Do not rotate "tool / solution / platform / offering" merely to dodge repetition; reuse the precise noun.
  Because: Precise repeated vocabulary is clearer than artificial synonym cycling.
- Rule: **Don't disguise a list as prose.** Avoid "The first… The second… The third…" paragraphs that are a bulleted list in a trench coat. Make it a real list or connect the ideas with logic.
  Because: Readers can follow real structure more easily than disguised list scaffolding.
- Rule: **No chatbot leftovers.** Cut conversational or reasoning leaks such as "Certainly!", "Great question!", "Let's dive in", "Let me break this down", "I hope this helps", and "In this article, we will."
  Because: These leftovers reveal a generic assistant voice instead of the author's voice.
- Rule: **Don't end on "challenges and opportunities."** Avoid the closing cliché that lists obstacles then pivots to upbeat potential. End on a concrete point.
  Because: A concrete ending gives readers a specific takeaway instead of a generic model-style coda.
- Rule: **Don't chop a list into paired fragments.** Avoid runs such as "Isolation and security. Cost and control. As well as speed and scale." Connect the ideas with real logic, vary the sentence shapes, or use an actual list. Never start a sentence with "As well as."
  Because: Uniform paired fragments make a list sound mechanical instead of preserving natural flow.
- Rule: **Bold-label list scaffolding.** Do not shape every bullet as `**Label:** explanation sentence.` Before: "**Scalability:** The platform grows with your needs." After: use prose, or bullets whose items vary in shape and carry the point themselves.
  Because: Uniform bold-label bullets are a widely recognized AI-formatting tell.
- Rule: **Colon-subtitle headlines and Title Case headings.** Avoid "Gerund Phrase: How X Does Y" titles such as "Unlocking Growth: How AI Is Transforming Small Business" and Title Case on every heading. Use sentence case; delete the colon and keep whichever half says something concrete.
  Because: Formulaic colon subtitles and title case are recurrent model-generated surface patterns.
- Rule: **Framing sandwich.** Do not restate the intro in the conclusion, and do not open sections with "In this section we'll explore…" then close with "As we've seen…" Delete any sentence that only restates; end on the last new fact, not a recap.
  Because: Repetition makes the argument restart instead of carrying readers forward.
- Rule: **Both-sides hedging.** Avoid symmetrical benefit/drawback boilerplate such as "While X offers significant benefits, it also presents challenges." Commit to a claim; if a caveat is real, make it concrete, such as "this breaks when N > 10k."
  Because: Generic balance obscures the writer's actual stance and reads like a model default.
- Rule: **No future-outlook coda.** Do not close with vague confident speculation such as "As the technology continues to evolve, X is poised to play an even greater role." Cut it, or attribute a real prediction to a named source with a date.
  Because: Unattributed future speculation damages factual trust and creates a generic ending.
- Rule: **Audience bracketing.** Do not open with the false-dichotomy reader address "Whether you're a seasoned developer or just starting out…" Address one real reader, or cut the clause and start with the substance.
  Because: Generic audience framing delays the point and signals a stock AI introduction.
- Rule: **Dictionary lead.** Do not define the title like a dictionary, such as "X refers to…" or "X is a term used to describe…" A crisp assertive definition such as "Customer churn is the rate customers leave" is fine; the limp *refers-to* phrasing is the tell.
  Because: Direct definitions help readers more than a generic dictionary-style opening.
- Rule: **Vary paragraph mass (paragraph-level uniformity).** Do not write runs of same-size 3–4 sentence paragraphs. Allow a one-sentence paragraph for emphasis, and let one run long where the argument needs it.
  Because: Perfect rectangles of paragraph text are a recognizable machine-writing pattern.
- Rule: **Knowledge-cutoff residue.** Cut model-uncertainty leftovers such as "Based on available information…", "While specific figures are not publicly available…", and bare "As of 2024" hedges with no source. A dated attributed fact such as "as of March 2026, per Ahrefs" is good; an unattributed hedge is the tell.
  Because: Unattributed uncertainty damages factual trust and exposes a model-generation artifact.
