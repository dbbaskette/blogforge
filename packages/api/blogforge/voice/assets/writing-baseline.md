- Rule: Prefer concrete details over abstractions. Name the thing, show the example, and replace "improves performance" with the actual number.
  Because: Concrete details help readers understand and trust the point.
- Rule: Earn every sentence. Cut a sentence that does not carry weight; shorter is usually stronger.
  Because: Readers can follow a focused argument without filler.
- Rule: Vary sentence length. Mix short and long sentences for rhythm instead of using a uniform cadence.
  Because: Varied rhythm keeps the prose readable and prevents a machine-like cadence.
- Rule: Prefer one precise verb over an adjective-adverb stack; "slashed" beats "significantly reduced."
  Because: Precise language makes the action easier for readers to understand.
- Rule: Use specifics instead of claims. Numbers, names, and examples beat vague praise.
  Because: Specific evidence gives readers a clear basis for the claim.
- Rule: Keep one idea per sentence. Split any sentence trying to do two jobs.
  Because: A single clear idea per sentence reduces reader effort.
- Rule: Use active voice by default. Name who does what, and use passive voice only when the actor genuinely does not matter.
  Because: Clear actors and actions make the prose easier to follow.
- Rule: Cut hedging and filler, including "very," "really," "quite," "in order to," and throat-clearing intros.
  Because: Direct wording helps readers reach the point quickly.
- Rule: Read the draft aloud. If a person would not say it that way, rewrite it; do not sound robotic.
  Because: Spoken cadence exposes phrasing that readers will find unnatural.
